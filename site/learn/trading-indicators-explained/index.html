Trading Indicators Explained: Moving Averages, VWAP, RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands, ATR and Volume

Published Nov 24, 2026. Part 3 of a three-part series on reading charts. Formulas and default settings come from StockCharts' ChartSchool, linked in each section. Educational only, not financial advice.

The short version

An indicator is a formula applied to price and volume. It does not know anything the chart does not already show; it just summarizes it in a different way. That makes indicators useful for answering specific questions, such as which way the trend is, how volatile the market is and whether volume is unusual. It also means stacking five indicators built from the same prices does not give you five independent opinions.

Moving averages: SMA and EMA

What it measures: the average price over a set number of bars, which smooths out noise so you can see the trend.

How it is calculated: a simple moving average (SMA) adds up the last N closes and divides by N. An exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent prices, using a multiplier of 2 / (N + 1). For a 10-period EMA that is 2 / 11, so the newest price gets about 18.18% of the weight (StockCharts).

Common uses: reading trend direction (price above a rising average, or below a falling one), spotting possible support and resistance, and crossover systems where a short average crosses a long one.

Common mistakes: forgetting that averages lag because they are built from past prices, and trading crossovers in a sideways market. When there is no strong trend, crossover systems produce many whipsaws (StockCharts). Longer averages lag more than shorter ones.

VWAP (volume-weighted average price)

What it measures: the average price traded during the session, weighted by how much volume traded at each price. It works almost like a one-day moving average.

How it is calculated: for each bar, take the typical price, (high + low + close) / 3, and multiply it by that bar's volume. Keep a running total of that, and a running total of volume. VWAP is the first total divided by the second. It is calculated from intraday data for a single day, starting at the open (StockCharts).

Common uses: gauging the intraday trend (price above or below VWAP), and judging fills. Institutions developed it to place large orders without disrupting the market, and a buy below VWAP is generally considered a good fill.

Common mistakes: using standard VWAP on daily or weekly charts, where it does not apply, and forgetting it resets each session. If you want a VWAP that starts from an earnings day or a big low, use an anchored VWAP instead (StockCharts). For futures, remember that an ETH chart starts the session at 6:00 PM ET the evening before, so its VWAP will differ from an RTH chart's.

RSI (Relative Strength Index)

What it measures: momentum: the speed and size of recent up moves compared with recent down moves. It moves between 0 and 100.

How it is calculated: average the gains and the losses over the last 14 bars (Wilder's default), and divide average gain by average loss to get RS. Then RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS). Developed by J. Welles Wilder (StockCharts).

Common uses: readings above 70 are generally called overbought and below 30 oversold. Traders also watch divergences, where price makes a new high but RSI does not, and centerline crosses of 50.

Common mistakes: selling just because RSI is above 70. Momentum oscillators can become overbought and stay that way in a strong uptrend, and the reverse in a downtrend (StockCharts). In a strong uptrend, RSI has tended to range between 40 and 90, according to Constance Brown's work cited by StockCharts.

MACD (Moving Average Convergence/Divergence)

What it measures: momentum, by tracking the gap between a fast and a slow moving average.

How it is calculated: the MACD line is the 12-period EMA minus the 26-period EMA. The signal line is a 9-period EMA of the MACD line. The histogram is the MACD line minus the signal line. Developed by Gerald Appel in the late 1970s (StockCharts).

Common uses: signal-line crossovers (the most common signal), crosses of the zero line (the 12 EMA crossing the 26 EMA) and divergences.

Common mistakes: using MACD to call overbought or oversold. It has no upper or lower limit, so it is not well suited to that. MACD values are also in price units, so they cannot be compared across stocks at different prices; StockCharts suggests the Percentage Price Oscillator for that (StockCharts). In choppy markets crossovers whipsaw, just like the averages MACD is built from.

Bollinger Bands

What it measures: volatility around a moving average. The bands widen when volatility rises and narrow when it falls.

How it is calculated: the middle band is a 20-period SMA. The upper band is the middle band plus 2 standard deviations of price, and the lower band is the middle band minus 2 standard deviations. Developed by John Bollinger (StockCharts).

Common uses: judging whether price is stretched relative to recent volatility, confirming W-bottoms and M-tops, and watching for a squeeze, when the bands get unusually narrow.

Common mistakes: treating a touch of the upper band as an automatic sell, or the lower band as an automatic buy. In a strong trend price can keep pushing along one band, which traders call walking the bands (StockCharts).

ATR (Average True Range), and how to size a trade with it

What it measures: volatility, meaning how far price typically travels in one bar, including gaps. It says nothing about direction (StockCharts).

How it is calculated: first find each bar's true range, the largest of: today's high minus today's low, today's high minus yesterday's close, or today's low minus yesterday's close (as positive numbers). ATR is a smoothed average of the true range, usually over 14 bars. Wilder introduced it in his 1978 book New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems (StockCharts).

Common uses: setting stops that fit the market's normal movement, such as ATR-based trailing stops (StockCharts), and comparing how volatile a market is now versus last month.

Common mistakes: using a fixed dollar or point stop in every market. A $1 stop can be far too tight on a volatile stock and needlessly wide on a quiet one. ATR also changes over time, so the stop that fit last month may not fit today.

Sizing a trade with ATR: the stop distance comes from volatility, and the position size comes from how much you are willing to lose. Here is an illustrative example, not a recommendation:

Account$10,000
Risk per trade (1%)$100
Entry$50.00
14-day ATR$1.20
Stop at 1.5 x ATR below entry$50.00 - $1.80 = $48.20
Shares = $100 / $1.8055 shares (rounded down)

If volatility doubles and ATR becomes $2.40, the same rule puts the stop $3.60 away and the size drops to 27 shares. Your dollar risk stays at $100; only the size changes. That is the idea behind the 1% rule. Plug your own numbers into the position size calculator: enter your account, risk percentage, entry, and the ATR-based stop. For futures, the futures P&L calculator converts a stop in points into dollars per contract. Remember that gaps and fast markets can fill a stop worse than planned, so real losses can be larger.

Volume and relative volume (RVOL)

What it measures: how much is trading. Relative volume compares the current bar's volume with its normal level.

How it is calculated: RVOL = current volume / average volume. StockCharts uses a 50-period simple moving average of volume by default. A reading of 1.0 is normal volume, 2.5 means two and a half times normal, and readings above 4.0 are considered a volume spike (StockCharts).

Common uses: checking whether a breakout or a reversal has real participation behind it, and finding which stocks have unusual attention today.

Common mistakes: reading high volume as bullish or bearish on its own. Volume tells you how much traded, not which side won. Also be careful with RVOL on very thinly traded stocks, where a small number of trades can produce a big ratio.

The honest part

Every indicator here is a description of past prices and volume. None of them is an edge on its own. Published trading signals also tend to weaken once they are widely known: one study of 97 published stock-return predictors found returns 58% lower after publication (McLean and Pontiff, 2016). The research on candlestick patterns, covered in part 2, points the same way. Use indicators to describe the market clearly, then let a tested plan and sound risk management decide what you do. FAQ

Which indicator is the best? None on its own. Most popular indicators are calculated from the same prices, so they tend to agree with each other. Pick a few that answer different questions, such as trend, volatility and volume.

Does RSI above 70 mean I should sell? No. StockCharts notes that momentum oscillators can become overbought and stay overbought in a strong uptrend. Above 70 says price has risen fast, not that it must fall.

How do I use ATR for position sizing? Set your stop a multiple of ATR away from entry, then divide the dollars you are willing to lose by that stop distance. The position size calculator does the division for you.

Why does VWAP reset every day? Standard VWAP is calculated from intraday data for a single session, starting at the open. Anchored VWAP lets you start it from another point, such as an earnings day.
