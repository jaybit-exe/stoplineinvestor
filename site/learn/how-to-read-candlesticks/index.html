How to Read Candlestick Charts: OHLC, Wicks, Timeframes and Sessions

Published Nov 24, 2026. Part 1 of a three-part series on reading charts. Facts are linked to their sources in the text. Educational only, not financial advice.

The short version

A candlestick shows four prices for one slice of time: where price opened, the highest and lowest it traded, and where it closed. The thick part is the body, from open to close. The thin lines are the wicks (also called shadows), out to the high and the low. That is all a candle is. Everything else, including patterns and most indicators, is built from those four numbers.

Open, high, low, close

To draw a candlestick chart you need the open, high, low and close for each period. If price closes above where it opened, the candle is drawn hollow (or green on most screens), with the open at the bottom of the body and the close at the top. If it closes below the open, the candle is filled (or red), with the open at the top of the body. The top of the upper wick is the high and the bottom of the lower wick is the low (StockCharts ChartSchool).

Candlestick charting comes from Japan, where traders were already analyzing rice prices in the 17th century. According to Steve Nison, who introduced the method to Western traders, candlestick charts themselves appeared some time after 1850 (StockCharts).

What the body and wicks tell you

  • A long body means price moved a long way from open to close, with one side in control most of the period.
  • A small body means the open and close were close together. If the open and close are virtually equal, the candle is a doji.
  • A long upper wick means price traded well above where it closed. Buyers pushed it up, but it did not hold. A long lower wick is the opposite: sellers pushed price down and it came back (StockCharts pattern dictionary).

One catch: color only compares a candle's close with its own open. A green candle can still close below the previous candle's close if it opened on a big gap down. If you want to know whether price was up on the day, compare closes.

Timeframes: one candle, any length of time

A candle can cover one minute, five minutes, an hour, a day or a week. Longer candles are built from shorter ones: the first open, the highest high, the lowest low and the last close. That is why the same market can look calm on a daily chart and wild on a 5-minute chart. Nothing changed except how much time each candle summarizes.

A practical rule: decide which timeframe you make decisions on, and read patterns and levels on that one. A hammer on a 2-minute chart and a hammer on a weekly chart are not the same piece of information.

Sessions: why your futures candles may not match someone else's

US stocks have a core session from 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET, with early and late sessions around it on some venues. NYSE Arca, for example, runs an early session from 4:00 AM and a late session to 8:00 PM ET (NYSE hours). E-mini S&P 500 futures trade on CME Globex from Sunday 6:00 PM to Friday 5:00 PM ET, with a daily maintenance break from 5:00 to 6:00 PM ET (CME Group contract specs).

So a futures trader has a choice. An extended-hours (ETH) chart includes the overnight trading, so the daily candle opens at 6:00 PM ET the evening before. A regular-hours (RTH) chart only uses the daytime session, which many futures traders set to match the stock market's 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET. The two charts can draw very different candles for the same day:

  • Gaps show up on RTH charts, because the overnight move happens off the chart. On a 24-hour ETH chart, most of those gaps are filled in by overnight candles.
  • Highs and lows differ. "Yesterday's low" can be one number on an ETH chart and another on an RTH chart. If your stops or levels use the prior day's high or low, write down which session they come from.
  • Indicators change too. A 20-period average or an ATR on an ETH chart includes the quiet overnight bars, so it will not match the same setting on an RTH chart.

Neither setting is right or wrong. The mistake is mixing them: drawing levels on one and trading off the other.

Common mistakes when reading candles

  • Reading a candle before it closes. Until the period ends, the high, low and close can still change. A hammer at 3:45 PM can be a plain down candle by 4:00 PM.
  • Ignoring what came before. The same shape means different things after a rally and after a selloff. That is the whole point of the next article in this series.
  • Treating every wick as a signal. A long wick printed on very little volume tells you less than one printed on heavy volume. Check volume before reading much into it.
  • Comparing charts with different settings. Timeframe, session and data feed all change the candles.

FAQ

What do the four prices in a candlestick mean? Open is the first trade of the period, close is the last, high is the highest trade and low is the lowest. The body spans open to close and the wicks reach the high and low.

Does a green or hollow candle mean price went up for the day? It means the close was above the open for that candle. Price can still be lower than the previous candle's close if the candle opened on a gap down.

Should I use RTH or ETH charts for futures? Either can work, but pick one and use it consistently. The two settings give different opens, highs, lows and gaps, so levels like the prior day high depend on the session you chose.

When is a candle final? Only when its period ends. Until then the high, low and close can still change, so a pattern on a live candle can disappear before it closes.
