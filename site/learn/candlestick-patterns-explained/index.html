Basic Candlestick Patterns Explained, and What the Research Says

Published Nov 24, 2026. Part 2 of a three-part series on reading charts. Pattern definitions follow StockCharts' ChartSchool, and the research section links each study. Educational only, not financial advice.

The short version

Candlestick patterns are names for common shapes that one, two or three candles make. They describe what just happened, such as a selloff that got bought back or a rally that stalled. They do not tell you what happens next. The best academic tests found little or no predictive value in US and Japanese stocks, so use patterns as context and as places to put a stop, not as buy and sell signals on their own.

New to candles? Start with how to read candlesticks.

Two rules before any pattern

  • Context first. Most patterns are defined by the trend before them. A hammer and a hanging man are the same shape; one follows a decline and the other follows an advance (StockCharts).
  • Wait for confirmation. StockCharts treats bullish reversal patterns as neutral without confirmation, such as a gap up, a long up candle or a high-volume advance within 1 to 3 days. It also notes that candlestick patterns are short-term and usually effective for only 1 or 2 weeks (StockCharts).

The basic patterns

In the diagrams, the faded candles set up the trend and the solid candles are the pattern. Hollow candles closed up and filled candles closed down.

Doji

Open and close are virtually equal, so the body is a thin line. Alone, a doji is neutral: it shows a standoff. A long-legged doji has long wicks on both sides, a dragonfly doji opens and closes at the high, and a gravestone doji opens and closes at the low (StockCharts).

Hammer and hanging man

Small body near the top, long lower wick, little or no upper wick. Bulkowski's guideline is a lower wick at least two to three times the body (ThePatternSite). After a decline it is called a hammer; the same shape after an advance is a hanging man (StockCharts).

Shooting star and inverted hammer

Small body near the bottom and a long upper wick: price ran up and fell back. After an advance it is a shooting star; the same shape after a decline is an inverted hammer. Only the trend before it and what happens next decide whether it means anything (StockCharts).

Engulfing

Two candles. The second body completely covers the first body and closes in the opposite direction. After a downtrend it is bullish engulfing; after an uptrend, bearish engulfing (StockCharts).

Harami

Two candles. A small body sits completely inside the previous candle's body and is the opposite color. Harami means pregnant in Japanese. If the small candle is a doji, it is a harami cross (StockCharts).

Morning star and evening star

Three candles. A morning star is a long down candle, a small candle that gaps lower, then a long up candle that closes above the midpoint of the first body. The evening star is the mirror image at the top of an advance (StockCharts).

Inside bar and outside bar

These compare ranges, not bodies. An inside bar has a lower high and a higher low than the bar before it, so it fits inside the previous range. An outside bar has a higher high and a lower low, so it covers the previous range (TrendSpider). In the diagram, the second bar is an outside bar and the third is an inside bar.

What the research says

Candlestick patterns have been tested many times. The results are not kind to them.

StudyMarket and periodFinding
Marshall, Young and Rose (2006), Journal of Banking and Finance35 Dow Jones Industrial Average stocks, 1992 to 2002, 28 candlestick rulesNo value. 22 of the 28 rules were not statistically significant on any of the 35 stocks, and the authors concluded investors are unlikely to benefit.
Marshall, Young and Cahan (2008), Review of Quantitative Finance and AccountingJapanese stocks, 1975 to 2004Not profitable over the full 30 years, in any of three 10-year sub-periods, or in bull or bear markets, even in the country where candlesticks began.
Lu, Shiu and Liu (2012), Review of Financial Economics (PDF)Taiwan Top 50 stocks, Oct 2002 to Dec 2008, with a 2009 to 2011 out-of-sample checkThree bullish two-day patterns (piercing, bullish engulfing, bullish harami) were profitable after taxes and commissions, holding until an opposite pattern appeared. The three bearish patterns were not. Some samples were small: the piercing pattern had 11 trades.

So the strongest tests on large US and Japanese stocks found nothing, and the positive result comes from one market, one period and a specific way of holding the trade. That is a reason for curiosity, not confidence.

Practitioner statistics: Bulkowski

Thomas Bulkowski publishes performance statistics for 103 candle types on ThePatternSite, from his book Encyclopedia of Candlestick Charts. His numbers measure which way price breaks out of the pattern, not whether a trade made money. Several patterns behaved opposite to their textbook meaning more often than not:

Read these with care. Bulkowski says his figures are based on "hundreds of perfect trades", not real fills with commissions and slippage, and they come from his own testing rather than peer-reviewed research. He notes himself that a 60% hit rate for the hammer is "not far from random (50%)" (hammer page). His glossary defines a reversal as price entering and leaving a pattern in opposite directions (glossary).

Why published edges shrink

Even rules that do test well tend to fade. A study of 97 published stock-return predictors found their returns were 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower after publication (McLean and Pontiff, Journal of Finance, 2016). That study is about stock-selection signals in general, not candlesticks, but the lesson carries over: a pattern in a book is a pattern everyone else has read.

How to use patterns sensibly

  • As context, not a trigger. A hammer at a level you already cared about is more interesting than a hammer in the middle of nowhere. The level and your plan do the work; the candle adds a detail.
  • As a stop location. Patterns give natural invalidation points, such as just below a hammer's low or above a shooting star's high. Then size the trade so being wrong costs a fixed amount, using the position size calculator.
  • Test before you trust. Log every pattern trade in a journal, including the ones you skipped, and check your results with the expectancy calculator. A win rate means nothing without the average win and average loss next to it.
  • Count your costs. Short-term pattern trading means many round trips. Spreads, commissions and slippage add up quickly.

FAQ

Do candlestick patterns work? Not reliably on their own. A large test on 35 Dow stocks from 1992 to 2002 and a 30-year test on Japanese stocks found no value. One study of Taiwan stocks found three bullish two-day patterns profitable after costs. Treat patterns as context, not signals.

What is the difference between a hammer and a hanging man? Nothing in the shape. Both have a small body and a long lower wick. A hammer appears after a decline and a hanging man after an advance.

What is the difference between an engulfing pattern and an outside bar? An engulfing pattern compares candle bodies: the second body covers the first and closes in the opposite direction. An outside bar compares ranges: the second bar has a higher high and a lower low, whatever its open and close.

How should I use candlestick patterns? As a reason to look closer and as a place to put a stop, inside a plan you have already tested. Then size the trade from that stop, not from how good the pattern looks.
